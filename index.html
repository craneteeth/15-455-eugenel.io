Overview

Suppose you must find the one input out of $N$ that makes some function $f$ output $1$, and the only thing you can do with $f$ is evaluate it. Classically there is nothing better than trying inputs one by one, which takes about $N$ evaluations. In 1996 Grover showed that a quantum computer needs only about $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N$ . A year later, Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani proved that no quantum algorithm can do asymptotically better .

Together, these two results are one of the cleanest stories in quantum complexity theory, and the subject of this project. They give a speedup that applies to every problem in $\NP$, a matching lower bound that we can actually prove (rare in complexity theory, where we cannot even separate $\Ptime$ from $\NP$), and an explanation of why quantum computers are not expected to solve $\NP$-complete problems efficiently.

The notes are written for a 455 student who has seen Lectures 7–9 on quantum computation. They are the equivalent of two lectures, with most of the weight on the complexity-theoretic side.

Try Grover's Key Idea

Start with eight candidates and one marked input. The phase oracle flips only the marked amplitude's sign; the diffusion operator then reflects every amplitude about their average. This changes the marked amplitude from $1$ to $5/2$ in course units after one iteration, so its probability rises from $1/8$ to $25/32$. Follow each step below and compare the bar heights.

Where This Fits

The course built up the classes $\Ptime\subseteq\BPP$ and $\Ptime\subseteq\NP\subseteq\PSPACE$. Efficient quantum computation, the class $\BQP$, sits above $\BPP$ and below $\PSPACE$ , in fact below $\PP$ . The big open question is its relation to $\NP$. Grover's algorithm is the natural attack: it speeds up exhaustive search for any $\NP$ problem. Part II shows the speedup is only quadratic and cannot be improved for black-box search, which yields an oracle relative to which $\NP\not\subseteq\BQP$. It also shows, by an oracle in the other direction, why such evidence can never become a proof without new, non-relativizing ideas.
