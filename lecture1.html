Part I: From Quantum Basics to Grover

Course project notes · CS455 Fall 2026 Builds on Lectures 7–9 · continues in Part II

Introduction

In Lecture 9 we met Grover's algorithm through the lens of the Hadamard transform. The key subroutine, ReflectAcrossAverage, sends every amplitude $\alpha + \delta$ to $\alpha - \delta$, where $\alpha$ is the average amplitude. Alternating it with "if $f$ then minus" made the amplitude of the hidden string grow like $1, 3, 5, 7, \dots$, and after about $\sqrt N$ rounds we could extract the answer with decent probability. That analysis was deliberately loose: every step said "basically", and we were promised a cleaner, geometric treatment later.

This page delivers that treatment and uses it to pin down the query complexity of search exactly. It is the first half of the project. Part II then asks what this algorithm means for complexity theory: where quantum computation sits among the classes we know, whether a quantum computer can solve $\NP$-complete problems efficiently, and why the answer "not by brute force" can actually be proved.

The plan for this page:

  1. rewrites the course's quantum instructions as operations on vectors, which is the language the rest of the project needs.
  2. defines unstructured search, the query model, and the classical baseline of $\Theta(N)$ queries.
  3. states Grover's algorithm and shows its state is always described by just two numbers.
  4. turns those two numbers into a point on a circle and shows each iteration is a rotation ().
  5. works through $N = 4$ and $N = 8$ by hand.
  6. proves the main result: $O(\sqrt{N/t})$ queries suffice ().

Quantum Basics, as Vectors

We only need a small slice of the quantum model from Lectures 7–9, rewritten in the vector notation used by the wider literature. Nothing here is new physics; it is bookkeeping that makes the later geometry and the lower-bound proof in Part II easy to state.

States

An $n$-qubit state assigns an amplitude to each of the $N = 2^n$ basic states $\ket{x}$, $x \in \{0,1\}^n$. In the course we kept amplitudes unnormalized: after "Add & Dif all" on fresh qubits every amplitude is $1$, the total squared amplitude is $N$, and "extract all" prints $x$ with probability proportional to the squared amplitude of $\ket x$. From now on we divide by the square root of the total, so that probabilities are the squared amplitudes themselves.

A state of $n$ qubits is a vector $\ket\psi = \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n} \alpha_x \ket x$ with $\sum_x \alpha_x^2 = 1$. "Extract all" prints $x$ with probability $\alpha_x^2$. The uniform state is

$$\ket s = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\ket x ,$$

the normalized version of what "Add & Dif all" produces from $\ket{0\cdots0}$.

The course only ever used real amplitudes, and so will we. Every argument on this site goes through unchanged for complex amplitudes if $\alpha_x^2$ is replaced by $\abs{\alpha_x}^2$.

We treat states as vectors in $\R^N$, with the usual inner product and length:

$$\braket{\varphi}{\psi} = \sum_x \varphi_x \psi_x, \qquad \norm{\ket\psi} = \sqrt{\braket{\psi}{\psi}} .$$

A state is just a vector of length $1$. We also use the shorthand $\ket{s}\!\bra{s}$ for the map $\ket v \mapsto \braket{s}{v}\ket s$, the projection onto the direction of $\ket s$.

Instructions are unitary

Lecture 7 observed that each instruction we use preserves the total squared amplitude, once "Add & Dif" and "Avg & Dev" are rescaled to the Hadamard instruction. Each instruction is also linear (it acts on a superposition by acting on each basic state and superposing the results). A linear map $U$ with $\norm{U\ket v} = \norm{\ket v}$ for all $v$ is called unitary. Unitaries also preserve inner products, because $\braket{u}{v} = \tfrac14\left(\norm{u+v}^2 - \norm{u-v}^2\right)$, so they preserve lengths, angles and distances.

Here is the dictionary between the course instructions used by Grover's algorithm and their vector form. Lemma references point to where each line is justified.

Unstructured Search and the Query Model

Brute-force search is the most basic algorithm in computer science: to find a satisfying assignment of a formula on $n$ variables, try all $2^n$ of them. In Lecture 9 this was the problem Unique-SAT, where $f$ is given as AND/OR/NOT code $C$ with $L$ lines. One evaluation of $f$ costs $O(L)$ steps, so brute force costs about $L\cdot 2^n$. The question here is how much better we can do if we refuse to look inside $C$ and only evaluate it.

Let $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$, let $N = 2^n$, and let $M = \{x : f(x) = 1\}$ be the set of marked strings, with $t = \abs{M}$. The search problem is: given access to $f$ with $t \ge 1$, output some $x \in M$. Unique search is the special case $t = 1$, where we write $x^*$ for the unique marked string.

A query is one application of the instruction "if $f$ then minus", that is, of $O_f$, to $n$ designated query qubits, while any other qubits are left alone. An algorithm in the query model may use any instructions it likes, but it may access $f$ only through queries. Its cost is the number of queries it makes.

A quantum query can act on a superposition of many strings at once. That is the whole source of the speedup, and also what makes lower bounds in Part II nontrivial.

There are two reasons. First, query bounds translate into time bounds: if $f$ is given by code with $L$ lines, each query can be implemented with $O(L)$ instructions (the course's recipe for turning classical code into "if $f$ then minus"), so an algorithm with $q$ queries and $r$ other instructions runs in time $O(qL + r)$. Second, and more important for a complexity class, in this model we can prove lower bounds. Proving that SAT needs exponential time is far beyond current techniques; proving that SAT needs many evaluations when the formula is a black box is the content of Part II.

One can also access $f$ through "if $f$ then toggle Ans". Nothing changes: Lecture 8's analysis of MysteryToggles showed that if Ans is first prepared with amplitude $+1$ on $\ket 0$ and $-1$ on $\ket 1$, then "if $f$ then toggle Ans" acts exactly as "if $f$ then minus" on the query qubits. Conversely "if $f$ then minus" is all Grover's algorithm uses. So the two query types cost the same up to a constant.

The classical baseline

For unique search on $N$ strings:

  1. every deterministic algorithm makes at least $N - 1$ queries on some input;
  2. every randomized algorithm that outputs $x^*$ with probability at least $2/3$ on every input makes at least $\tfrac23 N - 1$ queries on some input.

(1) Play adversary: answer $0$ to every query. If the algorithm stops after at most $N-2$ queries, at least two strings were never queried, and it outputs at most one of them. Mark the other one. Every answer we gave is consistent with this $f$, so the algorithm is wrong on it.

(2) A randomized algorithm is a probability distribution over deterministic ones, so it suffices to show that every deterministic algorithm $A$ with $q$ queries succeeds with probability at most $(q+1)/N$ when $x^*$ is uniformly random. Run $A$ with every answer equal to $0$. This determines a fixed list of queried strings $y_1,\dots,y_q$ and a fixed output $z$. On the actual input, $A$ follows the same run until a query hits $x^*$, so it can succeed only if $x^* \in \{y_1,\dots,y_q,z\}$, which has probability at most $(q+1)/N$. Averaging over the algorithm's random choices, if every input is solved with probability $\ge 2/3$ then so is a random input, hence $(q+1)/N \ge 2/3$.

So classically, search costs $\Theta(N)$ queries, and the next sections bring this down to $O(\sqrt N)$.

Grover's Algorithm

Here is the algorithm from Lecture 9, with the number of rounds left as a parameter $k$ to be chosen in .

  1. new qubit X1, …, Xn
  2. Add & Dif all # uniform superposition
  3. repeat $k$ times: # one "Grover iteration" per round
  4. if $f$(X1, …, Xn) then minus # one query
  5. ReflectAcrossAverage(X1, …, Xn)
  6. extract all

In vector form, the final state is $(D\,O_f)^k \ket s$. The algorithm makes exactly $k$ queries and $O(nk)$ other instructions.

To analyze it we first describe ReflectAcrossAverage as a vector operation. Lecture 9 proved, through the Hadamard transform, that it maps each amplitude $\alpha_x$ to $2\bar\alpha - \alpha_x$, where $\bar\alpha$ is the average amplitude.

ReflectAcrossAverage acts as $D = 2\ket s\!\bra s - I$. Equivalently, $D$ fixes $\ket s$ and negates every vector orthogonal to $\ket s$.

For $\ket\psi = \sum_x \alpha_x\ket x$ we have $\braket{s}{\psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_x \alpha_x = \sqrt N\,\bar\alpha$, so the $x$-coordinate of $2\ket s\!\braket{s}{\psi}$ is $2\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\cdot\sqrt N\,\bar\alpha = 2\bar\alpha$. Hence $(2\ket s\!\bra s - I)\ket\psi$ has $x$-coordinate $2\bar\alpha - \alpha_x$, which is Lecture 9's formula. For the second sentence: $D\ket s = 2\ket s - \ket s = \ket s$, and if $\braket{s}{v} = 0$ then $D\ket v = -\ket v$.

Note what "orthogonal to $\ket s$" means concretely: a vector is orthogonal to $\ket s$ exactly when its amplitudes average to $0$. So $D$ keeps the average and flips all the deviations from it, which is Lecture 9's picture again.

The next lemma is the structural fact that makes the whole analysis two-dimensional.

Suppose a state gives one common amplitude $a$ to every marked string and one common amplitude $b$ to every unmarked string. Then after "if $f$ then minus" and after ReflectAcrossAverage, the state again has this form. In particular, at every point of Grover's algorithm the state is described by a pair $(a, b)$.

"If $f$ then minus" sends $(a,b)$ to $(-a, b)$. For ReflectAcrossAverage, the average is $\bar\alpha = (ta + (N-t)b)/N$, and every amplitude $\alpha_x$ becomes $2\bar\alpha - \alpha_x$, which depends only on $\alpha_x$. So all marked strings get $2\bar\alpha - a$ and all unmarked strings get $2\bar\alpha - b$. The initial state $\ket s$ has the form with $a = b = 1/\sqrt N$, and induction finishes the proof.

Try it yourself in below. Press the two instructions one at a time and watch the dashed average line.

Right after the phase flip, the marked amplitude sits at about $-a$ while the average is still close to $b$. It is therefore about $a + b$ below the average, and reflecting puts it about $a + b$ above it. With $N$ large and $a$ small, each round adds roughly $2b \approx 2/\sqrt N$ to the marked amplitude, which is Lecture 9's $1, 3, 5, 7, \dots$ in course units. But each round also lowers the average a little, so the gains shrink. Eventually the average becomes negative and the marked amplitude starts to fall. The geometric view explains both effects at once.

The Geometric View

By , the $N$-dimensional state only ever moves in a two-dimensional plane. Choosing good coordinates in that plane turns Grover's algorithm into a rotation, and rotations are easy to iterate.

Assume $1 \le t \le N-1$. Define the unit vectors

$$\ket{G} = \frac{1}{\sqrt t}\sum_{x\in M}\ket x, \qquad \ket{B} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N-t}}\sum_{x\notin M}\ket x ,$$

the uniform superpositions over the good (marked) and bad (unmarked) strings. They are orthogonal, and span a plane $P$. Let $\theta \in (0, \pi/2)$ be the angle with $\sin\theta = \sqrt{t/N}$. For an angle $\varphi$, write $\ket{\varphi} = \cos\varphi\,\ket B + \sin\varphi\,\ket G$ for the unit vector in $P$ at angle $\varphi$, measured from $\ket B$ towards $\ket G$.

A state with amplitudes $(a, b)$ as in is $a\sqrt t\,\ket G + b\sqrt{N-t}\,\ket B$, a vector in $P$. In particular the starting state is

$$\ket s = \sqrt{\tfrac tN}\,\ket G + \sqrt{\tfrac{N-t}{N}}\,\ket B = \sin\theta\,\ket G + \cos\theta\,\ket B = \ket{\theta} .$$

The probability that "extract all" prints a marked string, from the state $\ket\varphi$, is the total squared amplitude on marked strings, namely $\sin^2\varphi$. So the goal is to turn the angle from $\theta$ (tiny when $t \ll N$) to about $\pi/2$.

On $P$, $O_f$ is the reflection across the line through $\ket B$: $O_f\ket\varphi = \ket{-\varphi}$.

$O_f$ negates every marked basic state, so $O_f\ket G = -\ket G$ and $O_f\ket B = \ket B$. Hence $O_f(\cos\varphi\ket B + \sin\varphi\ket G) = \cos\varphi\ket B - \sin\varphi\ket G = \ket{-\varphi}$.

$D$ maps $P$ to itself, and on $P$ it is the reflection across the line through $\ket s = \ket\theta$: $D\ket\varphi = \ket{2\theta - \varphi}$.

By , $D$ fixes $\ket\theta = \ket s$ and negates everything orthogonal to $\ket s$; in particular it negates $\ket{\theta + \pi/2}$, which lies in $P$ and is orthogonal to $\ket\theta$. Write $\ket\varphi = \cos(\varphi-\theta)\ket\theta + \sin(\varphi - \theta)\ket{\theta+\pi/2}$ (a rotation of coordinates by $\theta$). Applying $D$ gives $\cos(\varphi-\theta)\ket\theta - \sin(\varphi-\theta)\ket{\theta+\pi/2}$, the vector at angle $\theta - (\varphi - \theta) = 2\theta - \varphi$.

In a plane, let $R_\beta$ be the reflection across the line at angle $\beta$, so $R_\beta\ket\varphi = \ket{2\beta - \varphi}$. Then $R_\beta R_\gamma$ is the rotation by $2(\beta - \gamma)$: it sends $\ket\varphi$ to $\ket{\varphi + 2(\beta - \gamma)}$.

$R_\beta R_\gamma \ket\varphi = R_\beta\ket{2\gamma - \varphi} = \ket{2\beta - 2\gamma + \varphi}$. (The formula $R_\beta\ket\varphi = \ket{2\beta-\varphi}$ is the computation in the proof of with $\theta$ replaced by $\beta$.)

The oracle is the mirror at angle $0$ () and ReflectAcrossAverage is the mirror at angle $\theta$ (). By , one Grover iteration $DO_f = R_\theta R_0$ is a rotation by $2\theta$. Starting from angle $\theta$, we simply add $2\theta$ per round.

Let $1 \le t \le N - 1$ and $\sin\theta = \sqrt{t/N}$. After $k$ iterations the state is

$$(DO_f)^k\ket s \;=\; \sin\big((2k+1)\theta\big)\,\ket G + \cos\big((2k+1)\theta\big)\,\ket B ,$$

so "extract all" prints a marked string with probability exactly $\sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big)$, and then each marked string is equally likely.

Induction on $k$. For $k = 0$ the state is $\ket s = \ket\theta$. If after $k$ iterations the state is $\ket{(2k+1)\theta}$, then one more iteration gives $R_\theta R_0\ket{(2k+1)\theta} = \ket{(2k+1)\theta + 2\theta} = \ket{(2k+3)\theta}$ by the three lemmas. The probability statement is the remark after , and the last claim holds because all marked strings share one amplitude.

With one marked string, the amplitude on $\ket{x^*}$ in course units is $\sqrt N \sin((2k+1)\theta)$, and $\theta = \arcsin(1/\sqrt N) \approx 1/\sqrt N$. While $(2k+1)\theta$ is small, $\sin((2k+1)\theta) \approx (2k+1)\theta$, so the amplitude is about $\sqrt N\cdot(2k+1)/\sqrt N = 2k+1$. That is exactly Lecture 9's "basically $1, 3, 5, 7, \dots$". The word "basically" was the small-angle approximation $\sin y \approx y$, which breaks down when the angle nears $\pi/2$: the sine curve flattens, and past $\pi/2$ it decreases.

Query Complexity of Search

says the success probability is $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$. To maximize it we want $(2k+1)\theta$ as close to $\pi/2$ as an integer $k$ allows, so $k \approx \frac{\pi}{4\theta} - \frac12$. Taking the floor of $\pi/(4\theta)$ is a clean choice that never overshoots by more than one step.

Let $1 \le t \le N-1$ be known, let $\sin\theta = \sqrt{t/N}$, and run Grover's algorithm with $k^* = \lfloor \pi/(4\theta)\rfloor$ iterations. Then it outputs a marked string with probability at least $1 - t/N$, using

$$k^* \;\le\; \frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{\frac{N}{t}} \;=\; O\!\left(\sqrt{N/t}\right) \text{ queries.}$$

By definition of the floor, $\frac{\pi}{4\theta} - 1 \lt k^* \le \frac{\pi}{4\theta}$. Multiplying by $2\theta$ and adding $\theta$:

$$\frac\pi2 - \theta \;\lt\; (2k^*+1)\theta \;\le\; \frac\pi2 + \theta .$$

So the final angle is within $\theta$ of $\pi/2$, and since $\sin^2$ is symmetric about $\pi/2$ and decreasing in the distance from it (on this range),

$$\sin^2\big((2k^*+1)\theta\big) \;\ge\; \sin^2\!\Big(\frac\pi2 - \theta\Big) = \cos^2\theta = 1 - \frac tN .$$

For the query count, $\theta \ge \sin\theta = \sqrt{t/N}$, so $k^* \le \pi/(4\theta) \le \frac\pi4\sqrt{N/t}$.

Compare with : for one marked string, classical algorithms need about $N$ queries, Grover needs about $0.785\sqrt N$. For $N = 2^{40}$ that is roughly $10^{12}$ versus $8\times10^{5}$.

Unique-SAT on $n$ variables, with $f$ given as code with $L$ lines, can be solved by a quantum algorithm in time $O(L\cdot 2^{n/2})$ with success probability at least $1 - 2^{-n}$.

Apply with $t = 1$: $O(\sqrt N) = O(2^{n/2})$ queries, each implemented in $O(L)$ instructions, plus $O(n)$ instructions per iteration for ReflectAcrossAverage. Since $n \le L$, the total is $O(L\cdot 2^{n/2})$. The success probability is at least $1 - 1/N$.

Classically, extra queries never hurt. Here they do: the state keeps rotating, so the success probability is periodic in $k$ with period about $\pi/(2\theta)$ iterations, as shows. This is why the algorithm needs to know $t$ (or at least its order of magnitude) to stop at the right time. When $t$ is unknown, choosing the number of iterations at random fixes the problem; we prove this in because Part II needs it to search for $\NP$ witnesses.

Nothing above used the fact that the starting state was uniform. If $A$ is any quantum procedure (with no measurements) whose output is "good" with probability $p$, replace $\ket s$ by $A\ket{0\cdots0}$ and ReflectAcrossAverage by the reflection across $A\ket{0\cdots0}$, which is $A(2\ket{0\cdots0}\!\bra{0\cdots0} - I)A^{-1}$. The same two-mirror argument, with $\sin^2\theta = p$, gives a good output with high probability after $O(1/\sqrt p)$ runs of $A$ and $A^{-1}$, instead of the $O(1/p)$ repetitions needed classically . Grover search is the case where $A$ is "Add & Dif all" and $p = t/N$.

Summary

  1. Two mirrors make a rotation. "If $f$ then minus" and ReflectAcrossAverage are reflections of one plane, with mirrors at angle $\theta$, where $\sin\theta = \sqrt{t/N}$. Each iteration turns the state by $2\theta$.
  2. Exact formula. After $k$ iterations the success probability is $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$; Lecture 9's $1,3,5,7$ is the small-angle view of it.
  3. Quadratic speedup. About $\frac\pi4\sqrt{N/t}$ queries find a marked string with probability $\ge 1 - t/N$, versus $\Theta(N/t)$ classically.
  4. Stop on time. Unlike classical search, iterating too long hurts, because the rotation overshoots.

This leaves two questions for Part II. What does a quadratic speedup for search say about quantum computers and $\NP$? And could a cleverer quantum algorithm beat $\sqrt N$?
